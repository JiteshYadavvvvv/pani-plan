4.6 Problem Background

Farming uses most of India's fresh water. In many places, water comes from the ground through borewells and pumps. When a farmer gives more water than needed, the extra water is wasted, the pump uses more electricity, and the groundwater level goes down. When a farmer gives less water than needed, the plant gets "stressed" (thirsty) and the harvest becomes smaller.

The right amount of water depends on many things: the type of soil, the crop, how old the plant is, how hot it is and how much it rained. Scientists already have a trusted method to calculate this (called FAO-56), but it is written for experts. A farmer in a village usually never gets to use it. So the decision is made by habit or guesswork. That is why a simple, clear tool is needed.

4.8 DETAILED PROBLEM STATEMENT

In India, most farmers decide how much water to give their crops by habit, by guessing, or by copying their neighbours. Very few farmers measure their soil or check the weather before watering. This is a problem because India is a very large and varied country. The black soil of Maharashtra holds water differently from the sandy soil of Rajasthan or the alluvial soil of Punjab. Rice, wheat, cotton and sugarcane all need different amounts of water, and even the same crop needs more water when it is flowering than when it is a small seedling. Hot and dry days pull more water out of the soil and leaves than cool and cloudy days.

The people most affected are farmers, especially small farmers, along with the farm workers, agriculture officers and government planners who support them. The problem happens in villages across every state, wherever crops are watered from wells, borewells or canals.

Today, the most common method is flood irrigation on a fixed routine: the farmer fills the field with water every few days, whether the crop needs it or not. Scientists already have a trusted formula for crop water needs (the FAO-56 method), and the government and ICAR have published lots of soil and crop data. But this information is spread across different places and written for experts, not farmers. There is no simple tool that tells a farmer in one line: "Give about 18 mm of water every 6 days."

Because of this, a lot of water and electricity is wasted, groundwater levels keep falling, and crops sometimes suffer from too little water, which lowers the harvest and the farmer's income. Doing the scientific calculation for every field and every district also means a very large number of small calculations, which takes time on a normal computer. Therefore, there is a clear need for a simple, crop-wise and location-wise watering advice tool that uses trusted science, explains the answer in plain words, and can handle many calculations quickly using parallel computing, the same idea we saw at C-DAC.

4.10 Explanation of Existing Process

Step 1 - Input: The farmer has a field, a crop and a water source such as a well, canal or borewell.

Step 2 - Existing process: The farmer decides when and how much to water by habit, guess, or by asking a neighbour. No soil, weather or crop-stage information is used.

Step 3 - Activity (main problem): The field is watered on a fixed routine, often by flooding it. This is where the main problem happens, because the amount is not measured.

Step 4 - Decision: Was it the right amount at the right time? Usually nobody knows for sure.

Step 5 - Output: In the bad case, water and electricity are wasted, or the crop gets too little water and grows less. In the lucky case, the crop grows well, but the farmer cannot repeat it on purpose.

4.12 NEED FOR IMPROVEMENT

The current way of watering is not good enough because it is based on guesswork. What is needed is a simple helper that brings together soil, crop and weather information, uses a trusted scientific method to work out the right amount of water, and gives the answer in one easy line in the farmer's language. It should also be able to handle many fields or districts at once, quickly.

CHAPTER 5

LITERATURE SURVEY AND EXISTING SYSTEM STUDY

5.1 Objective of Literature Survey

A literature survey means reading what other researchers and engineers have already studied or built about our problem. It tells us which methods work, what data is needed, what results others got, and what is still missing. For this project we surveyed 31 research papers and authoritative sources, focusing heavily on recent publications from 2023, 2024, 2025, and 2026, alongside foundational guidelines from the United Nations FAO, national reports from C-DAC and the Central Ground Water Board, and our working web prototype (https://pani-plan.vercel.app/).

5.2 Literature Selection

We selected sources directly connected to our problem: how to calculate crop water needs with limited weather data, how to design simple digital advisory systems for farmers, why India's groundwater crisis requires urgent action, and how parallel computing speeds up batch calculations across thousands of fields. We prioritized recent peer-reviewed journals from 2023 onwards, including Agricultural Water Management, Computers and Electronics in Agriculture, Water, Agronomy, IoT, and Array (Elsevier), as well as the updated FAO-56 Rev. 1 guidelines (2024) and recent Central Ground Water Board surveys (2023-2024).

5.6 DETAILED LITERATURE DISCUSSION

Theme 1: How to calculate crop water needs

The FAO-56 standard [28] is the worldwide 'rule book' for working out how much water a plant drinks. Think of it like this: first, we find out how 'thirsty' the sunny, dry air is on any day-this is called reference evapotranspiration (ETo). It is like measuring how quickly wet laundry dries on a sunny clothesline. Then, we multiply that number by a crop factor (Kc), because a big, leafy sugarcane plant drinks much more water than a tiny baby sprout! The full FAO-56 formula needs eight different weather readings, like wind speed and sunshine hours, which most Indian villages do not have. Andrade and Ventura [1], Kim et al. [4], and Zúñiga-Alarcón et al. [21] proved between 2023 and 2024 that a simpler formula (called Hargreaves-Samani), which only needs daily high and low temperatures and the farm's location, gives great results that are more than 90% accurate when compared to the full method. This confirms that using the Hargreaves-Samani formula in our PaaniPlan backend is scientifically sound and perfect for Indian farmers.

Theme 2: Advice systems for farmers and why water matters

Having a good formula is not enough-the advice must reach the farmer in words they can actually understand. In 2023, Vallejo-Gómez et al. [6] surveyed 120 smart irrigation tools and discovered that the number one reason farmers stop using agritech apps is that the screens are too complicated and full of confusing science words! Dong et al. [11] and Garcés-Sánchez et al. [19] found in 2024 that farmers do not want confusing graphs of soil pressure; they just want to know: 'How much water should I give, and on what day?' In Morocco, Ezzahri et al. [2] built a website called Soqia-Advice that helped farmers save 22% of their water. In Portugal, Silva et al. [22] showed that farmers using web advice saved water year after year. Meanwhile, reports from the Central Ground Water Board [14, 15] and research by Banerjee et al. [12] and Rana et al. [13] show that 87% of all pumped water in India is used for farming, and over 60% of blocks in northwestern states are over-pumping their wells. These recent studies prove that building an easy, bilingual website like PaaniPlan [31] can make a real difference in saving India's precious groundwater.

Theme 3: Making many calculations fast (parallel computing)

India has over 140 million farms. If we want to calculate custom watering schedules for thousands of villages every morning, doing them one by one in a standard computer loop takes too long! At C-DAC Pune [30], we saw how supercomputers solve giant problems by splitting the work across many processors. In computer science, this is called parallel computing. Imagine 10,000 exam sheets: if one teacher grades them alone, it takes days; if four teachers share the pile, they finish much faster! However, as Adnan [3], Karbowski [24], and Benilov and Benilova [26] showed in recent 2023-2025 papers, there is a famous rule called Amdahl's Law: because some parts of the work cannot be split, and because handing out the tasks takes extra time (overhead), you cannot speed up a program forever just by adding more processors! In fact, for tiny jobs of only 100 fields, splitting the work across many processors actually makes it slower because of setup lag. Harris et al. [29] and Rauber and Rünger [25] show that using NumPy array vectorization lets the computer calculate 15,000 fields in a single mathematical heartbeat. This is why PaaniPlan includes a live Parallel Benchmarking Lab, letting anyone see the real difference between serial loops, multi-core pools, and vector math.

5.7 Overall Findings from Literature

Across all 31 studies from 2023 to 2026, four clear conclusions stand out: (1) The science of crop water needs is well proven, and using temperature-based formulas (Hargreaves-Samani) is accurate enough when village weather data is limited. (2) Websites are an effective way to reach farmers, but the screen must be clean, simple, and available in local languages like Hindi. (3) India's groundwater crisis is severe, with 87% of extraction driven by farming, making digital water scheduling a national priority. (4) Parallel computing can process thousands of farms in milliseconds, but programmers must be mindful of Amdahl's Law and thread overhead. The biggest gap in existing work is that almost all previous tools were either made for experts, made for other countries, or too expensive for smallholder farmers.

5.11 IDENTIFIED GAP

After reading the papers and checking existing tools, we found these gaps:

No simple answer: Existing tools give charts and numbers for experts, but not one clear line like "give 18 mm every 6 days".

Data is scattered: Soil data (ICAR), crop data (FAO) and weather data (IMD/government) are kept in different places.

Not made for India: Good advice websites exist, but for other countries and crops.

Language: Most tools are only in English; farmers need Hindi and other Indian languages.

Scale: Powerful computing (like at C-DAC) is used for science, but not for everyday farm advice for many districts at once.

5.12 REFINED FINAL PROBLEM STATEMENT

Farmers in India decide how much water to give their crops mostly by habit, which wastes water and electricity or harms the crop. A trusted scientific method (FAO-56 with the Hargreaves-Samani formula) already exists, and soil, crop and climate data are available, but they are scattered and written for experts. Existing tools such as CROPWAT are too complex, apps like Meghdoot give only general advice, and web advisory platforms are built for other countries. There is a need for a simple, India-focused web tool that takes the district, crop, growth stage, irrigation method and field size, and gives one plain-language answer in English or Hindi on how much water to give and how often. Because advice for many farms means many separate calculations, the tool should also show how parallel computing (multi-core and vectorised) can do them faster, and honestly show the limits of that speed-up.

6.3 INITIAL SYSTEM FLOW

Figure 6.1: Simple system flow of PaaniPlan (User → Website → Calculation → Answer)

Figure 6.2: Architecture of the PaaniPlan prototype. The dashed box shows parts that are only planned, not built.

In simple words: the user fills a small form on the website. The website checks the answers and sends them to the server. The server does four steps of maths: (1) works out how much water the air pulls out of the soil and plant each day (ETo); (2) multiplies it by the crop factor to get the crop's need (ETc); (3) subtracts useful rain; (4) divides by how efficient the watering method is (drip wastes the least, flood wastes the most). The soil type decides how many days to wait between waterings. The answer comes back as one simple line.

CONCLUSION AND FUTURE SCOPE

7.1 Conclusion

This project took us on a full learning journey. We started by studying 15 Computer Engineering jobs and the skills they need. Then we visited C-DAC, Pune, where we saw India's own supercomputers and learned how huge problems are solved by splitting them into many small parts. From what we saw, we listed three real problems and gave them marks, and the farm-water problem scored the highest.

We studied who is affected (farmers, workers, officers and planners) and how watering is done today, mostly by guesswork. We read 10 research papers and compared tools like CROPWAT, Meghdoot and Soqia-Advice. We found the gap: the science exists, but there is no simple, India-focused tool that gives a farmer one clear answer. To fill this gap, we built PaaniPlan, a working website that gives plain-language watering advice in English and Hindi and shows how parallel computing can make many calculations faster. The prototype uses sample data, so the next step is to connect real data and test it with farmers.

7.2 Future Scope

Connect live weather data and verified government soil and crop data in place of the sample dataset.

Test the advice on real farms and get it checked by agriculture experts.

Send daily advice by SMS or WhatsApp to farmers who do not use websites.

Add more Indian languages such as Marathi, Tamil and Telugu.

Use soil-moisture sensors (IoT) to measure the field directly.

Use machine learning to improve the advice for each local area.

Run early-morning batch advice for many districts on bigger servers or HPC systems like those at C-DAC.
